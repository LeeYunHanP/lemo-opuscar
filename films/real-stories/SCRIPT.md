# The Real Stories Behind GTA 6 — script for approval

Long-form YouTube documentary · 1920×1080 (16:9) · 24 fps · about 9 minutes
Look: found footage. Spliced 1990s camcorder tapes whose on-screen date and time carry the story; nothing claims to be real footage. No game footage, logos or real faces.
Voice: the same low, calm narrator as the shorts (Kokoro `am_onyx`), with closed captions. No music, only the sound each tape would have recorded.
Narration: about 1,260 words, which runs about 8½–9 minutes with chapter cards and pauses.

Each section gives the narration (what is spoken) and, below it, what's on screen.

> GTA 6 takes us back to Vice City, in a state called Leonida. It's a game. But almost everything in it is drawn from a real place: South Florida.

## Chapter 1 — Cocaine Cowboys (≈ 0:45–2:45)

> July 11th, 1979. Two-thirty in the afternoon. Dadeland Mall, south of Miami, one of the busiest shopping centers in the city.
> A delivery van pulls up outside a liquor store. Painted on its side: Happy Time Complete Party Supply.
> Two men walk in. Seconds later, the gunfire starts. The target is a Colombian trafficker who shops there regularly. He is killed, and so is the man with him.
> The shooters run back to the van and drive away. Police find it later, abandoned at the far end of the parking lot, with guns and bulletproof vests inside. They call it a war wagon.
> The first seven months of 1979 were called the bloodiest in South Florida's history. Cocaine was pouring in, and so was the money. Rivals settled scores in public, in daylight, with automatic weapons. The newspapers gave the new killers a name: the Cocaine Cowboys.
> By 1981, the county morgue had run out of room, and the medical examiner leased a refrigerated truck to hold the dead. That November, Time magazine put South Florida on its cover, under two words: Paradise Lost.
> A few years later, television turned the same city into pastel suits and neon. That's the Vice City most of us know. The real one came first.

*On screen:* a home-video tape in a mall parking lot (OSD JUL 11 1979). A box van with a hand-lettered party-supply sign rolls past. The camera stays outside and far away: a shop front, a broken window, people running. Tear. A newsstand rack of newspapers (invented mastheads). Tear. A hospital-style corridor with a parked refrigerated truck seen through a window. Tear. A magazine on a table, its cover shown only as an unreadable blur with "PARADISE LOST?" legible. Ends on a neon storefront at night.

## Chapter 2 — The Boatlift (≈ 2:45–4:40)

> April 1980. In Havana, a bus crashes through the gate of the Peruvian embassy. Within days, about ten thousand Cubans crowd into its gardens, asking to leave.
> Fidel Castro answers with a surprise: anyone who wants to go can go, from the port of Mariel.
> Boats from Florida race across the straits to pick them up: fishing boats, pleasure cruisers, anything that floats. Between April and October, around a hundred and twenty-five thousand people arrive in South Florida.
> Most were ordinary families. But Cuba also put people from its prisons and mental hospitals on the boats, and that small group shaped how the whole exodus was seen.
> Miami had to take in a small city's worth of people in six months. Newcomers slept in tent camps and in a football stadium. In the same weeks, riots tore through parts of the city.
> Hollywood took the story and ran. A gangster film made its fictional hero a Mariel refugee who rises through the cocaine trade, and it became a major inspiration for the original Vice City.
> But the real Mariel isn't that movie. It's tens of thousands of families who built a new Miami: the city whose food, music and Spanish-speaking streets you'll see in Leonida.

*On screen:* a tape filmed from a crowded boat deck at sea (OSD APR 1980): horizon, wake, a line of small boats. Tear. A harbor at dusk. Tear. Rows of tents under an elevated highway, cots, no faces. Tear. A street of storefronts with hand-painted Spanish signs (invented names), morning light.

## Chapter 3 — The Swamp (≈ 4:40–6:45)

> From the air, the Everglades look like a field that never ends: sawgrass, water and sky. It isn't really a swamp. It's a slow, shallow river, flowing south across the bottom of Florida. Much of what's left is protected inside a national park, dedicated in 1947.
> May 11th, 1996. ValuJet Flight 592 takes off from Miami just after two in the afternoon, heading for Atlanta with 110 people on board. Minutes later, the crew reports smoke. They try to turn back. They never make it.
> At 2:13, about ten minutes after takeoff, the plane dives into the Everglades at high speed, about seventeen miles northwest of the airport.
> Rescuers arrive to find almost nothing. No big pieces of wreckage. No survivors. Just jet fuel floating on the water between the sawgrass. Recovery teams worked in waist-deep mud, with marksmen watching for alligators and snakes.
> Investigators traced the fire to the cargo hold. A maintenance contractor had shipped chemical oxygen generators, expired and without their safety caps, listed on the paperwork as empty. They were not empty. Once one went off, it fed the fire with oxygen.
> After the crash, the FAA ordered airlines to fit fire detection and suppression in that kind of cargo hold. The rules for what planes may carry, and how, were rewritten because of what investigators found in that swamp.

*On screen:* reuses and extends the Everglades reel's tapes: aerial sawgrass from a small plane, the terminal window and the jet climbing out, the empty cockpit in the smoke test, the far shore and the distant dive, the airboat search, the fuel sheen, and the cargo notice. New: recovery tape, with search boats and orange markers on the water at a distance, no people close.

## Chapter 4 — Florida Man and the End of the Road (≈ 6:45–8:45)

> Every GTA game has its strange characters. Florida supplies its own. Since 2013, an online account has collected real headlines that start with two words: Florida Man.
> Journalists often credit Florida's open-records laws, which make arrest reports and mugshots easy to get. But other states have similar laws. The simpler answer may be that Florida is big, strange, and very well documented.
> Drive south from Miami and the mainland runs out. The Overseas Highway hops across a chain of islands to Key West, which is closer to Havana than it is to Miami.
> In April 1982, the Border Patrol set up a roadblock on that highway near Florida City, searching every car for drugs and undocumented migrants. Traffic backed up for miles. Tourists stopped coming. Key West asked, petitioned and sued. Nothing worked.
> So on April 23rd, at noon in Mallory Square, the mayor of Key West read out a proclamation. The Keys were seceding from the United States. The new nation was called the Conch Republic. As the story goes, it surrendered almost at once, and asked for foreign aid.
> The roadblock was soon gone. The Conch Republic still celebrates its independence every April.

*On screen:* a TV in a dim motel room showing a local-news screen of headlines (invented text, no real names, no mugshots). Tear. A car dashboard tape on the Overseas Highway: long bridges, turquoise water, a traffic jam of brake lights. Tear. A sunny town square with a crowd seen from far behind, a small blue flag on a pole (an original drawing, not the real flag design). Tear. A sunset over the water at the end of the road.
